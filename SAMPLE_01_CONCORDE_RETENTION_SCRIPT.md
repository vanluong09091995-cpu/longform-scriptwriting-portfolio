# FLAGSHIP SAMPLE V3 — Concorde Was a Better Machine Than a Business

**Format:** 9–11 minute YouTube documentary  
**Category:** aviation / technology / business  
**Purpose:** portfolio sample for research-led documentary writing

---

## 0:00–0:45 — THE LAST SCHEDULED FLIGHT

**[VISUAL]** Concorde at JFK, passengers boarding, departure board, then BA002 arriving at Heathrow.

On 24 October 2003, British Airways flight BA002 arrived at Heathrow from New York and closed the book on scheduled Concorde service.

For an aircraft that had spent its career outrunning clocks, the ending was almost ordinary: passengers walked off, cameras flashed, and a fleet capable of cruising at roughly twice the speed of sound stopped carrying scheduled passengers for good.

Concorde had been in commercial service since 1976. It could fly at up to 60,000 feet, cross the Atlantic in a fraction of the time of a conventional airliner, and offer an experience no other scheduled passenger aircraft could match.

The machine worked.

That is what makes its disappearance interesting.

Concorde was not retired because engineers suddenly discovered that supersonic travel was impossible. It was retired after decades of proving the opposite. The harder problem was building an airline business around a machine whose greatest strength — extreme performance — made almost everything around it more expensive, more specialised and harder to scale.

---

## 0:45–2:20 — AN AIRPLANE BUILT AROUND MACH 2

**[VISUAL]** Side profile, Olympus engine close-up, speed/altitude graphic, cabin cutaway.

British Airways' published specifications put Concorde's cruising speed at about 1,350 miles per hour, or Mach 2, with a cruising altitude of up to 60,000 feet. Four Rolls-Royce/SNECMA Olympus 593 engines provided the thrust, and the aircraft carried just 100 passengers.

Those numbers sound like the brochure for a technological triumph, because they were. But airlines do not earn money by winning engineering competitions. They earn it by filling seats often enough, on enough routes, at fares that cover the aircraft, crews, fuel, maintenance, airports and the industrial system behind the fleet.

Concorde made that arithmetic unusually demanding.

British Airways listed fuel consumption at roughly 25,600 litres per hour. The aircraft's cabin, meanwhile, was smaller than the cabins on many ordinary narrow-body jets. A flight therefore had a limited number of seats over which to spread the cost of operating one of the most specialised passenger aircraft ever built.

The same engineering choices that made Concorde fast also made it difficult to operate like a normal airliner. Supersonic performance was not simply a feature added on top of an ordinary business model; it shaped the business model from the beginning.

---

## 2:20–3:55 — A PREMIUM MARKET WITH A HARD CEILING

**[VISUAL]** Ticket/fare graphic, 100-seat cabin, route map contracting toward New York.

There was a market for that speed. Executives, celebrities and wealthy travellers were willing to pay heavily for hours saved across the Atlantic, and Concorde became a symbol of a kind of travel that was deliberately exclusive.

The problem was the size of the market.

The Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum notes that in Concorde's later years an average round-trip ticket could cost more than $12,000. It also describes many flights as only about half full, with some passengers travelling as guests of the airlines or through upgrades.

High fares can compensate for a small cabin only while enough customers continue to value the time saving at that price. Once load factors soften, the economics become unforgiving because the aircraft cannot suddenly add another hundred seats or move into a mass-market fare bracket.

Its network also became narrower over time. Services to Washington, Rio de Janeiro, Caracas, Miami, Singapore and other destinations disappeared, leaving New York as the core transatlantic market for the remaining operation.

Concorde could cross an ocean with extraordinary speed. What it could not do was make the number of customers willing to buy that speed expand indefinitely.

---

## 3:55–5:15 — THE NETWORK PROBLEM

**[VISUAL]** Map with ocean routes highlighted; simple sonic-boom animation over land.

A normal long-haul aircraft becomes more useful as an airline can deploy it across more cities, seasons and demand patterns. Supersonic travel came with an extra constraint: the sonic boom.

Noise restrictions meant Concorde could not simply fly supersonically over every populated route and reproduce its transatlantic advantage everywhere. That limited the number of city pairs on which its most valuable capability could be used without compromise.

Scale was limited in another way too. Only 20 Concordes were built in total, including development aircraft, and regular passenger service ultimately belonged to Air France and British Airways.

That is a tiny installed base for such a complex machine.

Large aircraft families create an ecosystem around themselves: thousands of trained people, deep stocks of parts, multiple operators, large maintenance networks and suppliers with a continuing reason to support the type. Concorde had the opposite structure. Every year the fleet aged, the industry supporting it was serving only a handful of aircraft.

The Smithsonian later described operating costs rising as parts became more difficult to obtain. For a fleet this small, ageing did not just mean old airframes; it meant maintaining an entire specialist system for fewer and fewer revenue flights.

---

## 5:15–6:55 — THE 2000 CRASH

**[VISUAL]** BEA report cover, runway diagram, aircraft photo. Keep the treatment factual and restrained.

On 25 July 2000, Air France Flight 4590 began its takeoff from Paris Charles de Gaulle.

The French BEA investigation found that the front-right tyre on the left main landing gear ran over a metal strip on the runway. The tyre was damaged, debris struck the aircraft, a fuel tank ruptured, and a major fire developed beneath the left wing. The Concorde crashed shortly after takeoff at Gonesse.

All 100 passengers and nine crew members aboard were killed, along with four people on the ground.

The disaster grounded Concorde and forced a major safety response. The fleet eventually returned to passenger service after modifications.

That return matters to the business story because it shows why the accident cannot be used as a complete explanation for the type's retirement. Concorde was not written off immediately; airlines and regulators spent the effort required to put it back into service.

What the accident changed was the burden of keeping an already small and expensive programme alive. Safety work, public confidence, engineering support and the economics of an ageing fleet were now being considered together, and there was very little scale available to absorb another major shock.

The crash was a catastrophe in its own right. Commercially, it landed on a business model that already had very little room for error.

---

## 6:55–8:25 — BY 2003, THE QUESTION HAD CHANGED

**[VISUAL]** Maintenance footage/photos, route board, hangar, preserved Concorde.

By the early 2000s, Concorde could still do the thing that made it famous. It could cruise at Mach 2, fly above normal airline traffic and deliver a transatlantic experience that no subsonic competitor could copy.

But the relevant question was no longer whether the aircraft could perform.

The question was whether it still made sense to maintain the people, parts, procedures and infrastructure required to keep such a small fleet operating.

Air France ended Concorde service in May 2003. British Airways followed in October, with BA002 from JFK to Heathrow becoming its final scheduled commercial flight.

There was no single moment when the laws of physics defeated Concorde. Instead, the business case narrowed from several directions at once: high operating costs, a small premium customer base, a limited route network, ageing aircraft and a specialist support system that was becoming harder to justify.

This is why the usual story — that one crash killed Concorde — is unsatisfying. It gives a dramatic event all the explanatory power and hides the slower forces that had been shaping the programme for years.

---

## 8:25–9:45 — WHAT ACTUALLY BEAT CONCORDE

**[VISUAL]** Split screen: Concorde on one side; busy subsonic widebody network, maintenance and gates on the other.

Concorde did have a faster supersonic passenger rival on paper: the Soviet Tu-144. Smithsonian records the Tu-144 at Mach 2.15 versus Concorde at Mach 2.04, while NASA records its passenger service as brief, ending in 1978 after 102 passenger flights.

What ultimately separated Concorde from that short-lived rival — and what later beat Concorde itself — was the operating advantage of being ordinary.

Subsonic airliners could carry more passengers, serve a far wider range of routes and belong to fleets large enough to support global maintenance and parts networks. Airlines could move them between markets, replace them with newer models and spread fixed costs across far more seats and flights.

None of that looked as futuristic as Mach 2, but it produced something airlines value even more than spectacle: flexibility.

Concorde had been optimised around an extraordinary capability. The rest of commercial aviation was being optimised around economics, network reach, capacity and repeatability.

Over time, those quieter advantages became decisive.

---

## 9:45–10:25 — ENDING

**[VISUAL]** Slow push on a preserved Concorde; finish on the nose/profile rather than a generic montage.

Today, Concorde sits in museums as evidence that the technology worked.

It carried paying passengers at roughly twice the speed of sound for more than a quarter-century. It made a seven-hour journey feel like a different category of travel and turned sustained supersonic airline service into something people could buy a ticket for.

Its weakness was not that it failed to deliver the product.

Its weakness was that the world required to support that product was too narrow.

A hundred seats, a tiny fleet, a premium customer base, difficult route economics and an increasingly specialised maintenance ecosystem all had to remain healthy at the same time.

Concorde proved that the future could arrive early.

It also proved that arriving early is not the same as becoming normal.
