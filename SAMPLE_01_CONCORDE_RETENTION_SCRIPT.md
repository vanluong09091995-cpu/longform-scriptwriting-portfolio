# PORTFOLIO SAMPLE 01
## Why Concorde Was Too Fast to Survive
Format: 10–12 minute YouTube documentary voiceover

### HOOK — 0:00–0:30
In 2003, commercial aviation did something almost unthinkable: it became slower on purpose.

For nearly three decades, one aircraft could leave London after breakfast and reach New York before the morning was over. Concorde crossed the Atlantic in under four hours, flew at roughly twice the speed of sound, and turned a seven-hour trip into something closer to a long lunch.

Then the fastest passenger aircraft in regular service disappeared.

Not because engineers forgot how to build it. Not because passengers stopped loving speed. And not because of one single crash.

Concorde died because the faster it flew, the narrower its business model became.

### PART 1 — THE MACHINE THAT BEAT TIME
Concorde entered commercial service in 1976 with Air France and British Airways. It was more than transportation. It was a promise that air travel would keep accelerating.

At cruise altitude, it flew between roughly 55,000 and 60,000 feet, high enough for passengers to see the curvature of Earth. The Atlantic could be crossed in less than four hours—about half the time of a conventional airliner.

That sounds like the future.

But the future had a problem hidden inside the physics.

Every minute Concorde saved in the air demanded more fuel, more specialized maintenance, more expensive parts, and a passenger willing to pay for all of it.

Speed was not the product. Speed was the cost structure.
### PART 2 — THE ECONOMICS WERE WORKING AGAINST IT
Concorde could carry about 100 passengers, but that did not mean every flight carried 100 paying premium customers.

Over time, many routes disappeared. Services to places such as Washington, Rio, Caracas, Miami and Singapore were cut back, leaving the core New York routes.

Smithsonian accounts of Concorde's later years describe flights that were often only half full. By the early 2000s, a round-trip ticket could average around $12,000.

That created a brutal equation.

The aircraft consumed enormous amounts of fuel while serving a tiny market of people for whom saving a few hours was worth a five-figure fare.

The more expensive Concorde became to operate, the higher the fare had to be. The higher the fare became, the fewer people could justify it. And the smaller the customer base became, the harder it was to spread those operating costs across enough seats.

Concorde was fast enough to defeat time—but not cheap enough to defeat basic economics.

### PART 3 — THE ROUTE NETWORK SHRANK
Supersonic travel also faced limits that normal airliners did not.

Noise and sonic-boom concerns restricted where supersonic flight could happen. That meant Concorde could not simply expand everywhere and compensate for its cost through scale.

Only 20 Concordes were built in total, and the type never became a mass-market global fleet.

Air France and British Airways were the only airlines to operate it in regular passenger service.

That mattered more with every passing year.

A huge Boeing or Airbus fleet creates an ecosystem: trained crews, spare parts, suppliers, maintenance knowledge and a large installed base that justifies continued support.

Concorde had the opposite problem. It was small, specialized, and getting older.
### PART 4 — THEN CAME THE 2000 CRASH
On July 25, 2000, Air France Flight 4590 crashed shortly after takeoff from Paris.

The accident was catastrophic, and regulators suspended Concorde's airworthiness certificates while investigators and operators worked on safety changes.

The fleet eventually returned to service after modifications, including measures intended to reduce the risk of fuel-tank damage and fire.

But the important point is this: the crash did not create Concorde's economic problem.

It exposed how fragile the entire system had become.

A normal aircraft family can absorb disruption because hundreds or thousands of aircraft still justify maintenance, training and investment.

Concorde had a tiny fleet, a shrinking route network, aging airframes and an already narrow premium market.

When a system has that little margin, one major shock matters far more.

### PART 5 — THE FINAL BUSINESS CASE
By 2003, the question was no longer, "Can Concorde still fly?"

It could.

The real question was, "Does it still make sense to keep the entire ecosystem alive?"

The answer was increasingly no.

Operating costs were high. Parts were becoming harder to source. The customer base was limited. The route network had contracted. The fleet was aging.

Air France ended Concorde service in May 2003. British Airways followed in October.

The aircraft was not retired because supersonic flight had become impossible.

It was retired because the economics of maintaining a tiny, expensive, specialized fleet no longer justified the speed it delivered.
### PART 6 — THE PARADOX OF CONCORDE
And that is what makes Concorde so interesting.

Technically, it succeeded.

It proved that civilian passengers could cross the Atlantic at twice the speed of sound in comfort. It turned a futuristic idea into a routine airline schedule for more than two decades.

Commercially, though, its greatest advantage was also its greatest constraint.

Concorde sold time—but only to people wealthy enough to value a few saved hours more than thousands of extra dollars.

That is not a large market.

So when fuel, maintenance, fleet age and operational complexity tightened the margins, there was nowhere for the business model to expand.

### ENDING — PAYOFF
Today, a flight from New York to London usually takes longer than it did for Concorde passengers decades ago.

That feels like technological regression.

But Concorde's story is not really about aviation moving backward.

It is about the difference between what engineering can make possible and what economics can keep alive.

Concorde was a masterpiece.

It was also a warning.

A product can be faster, more advanced and more impressive than everything around it—and still lose if the market required to sustain it is too small.

The machine beat time.

The business never beat the math.
