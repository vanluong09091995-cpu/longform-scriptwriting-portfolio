# PORTFOLIO SAMPLE 02
## The Year LEGO Nearly Broke Its Own Business
Format: 8–10 minute business/founder documentary voiceover

### HOOK — 0:00–0:30
In 2003, LEGO lost more than a billion Danish kroner in a single year.

Sales fell 26 percent. Pre-tax profit from the year before turned into a pre-tax loss of DKK 1.432 billion.

This was not a startup running out of cash.

This was LEGO — one of the most recognizable toy brands on Earth.

And the strangest part is that the company did not need a revolutionary new product to survive.

It needed to stop behaving like a company that could do everything.

### PART 1 — GROWTH HAD BECOME COMPLEXITY
By the early 2000s, LEGO was dealing with a toy market under pressure from electronics, changing retailers and aggressive global price competition.

But an external market problem is only dangerous when the inside of the business is already vulnerable.

LEGO's 2003 accounts showed the scale of that vulnerability: net sales fell from DKK 11.426 billion to DKK 8.433 billion.

A company can survive shrinking revenue if costs shrink with it.

LEGO's problem was that its cost base could not adjust quickly enough.
### PART 2 — THE TURNAROUND STARTED WITH SUBTRACTION
The 2004 annual report used unusually direct language: the company had to make fundamental changes if it was going to survive as an independent family-owned business.

The response was not "expand harder."

It was focus.

LEGO began separating core play-materials operations from activities it planned to sell, including its theme-park business.

It cut costs aggressively. It reduced assets. It restructured operations around the parts of the company that had to work.

This is where turnaround stories often look confusing from the outside.

In 2004, revenue still fell by 7 percent.

The full-year net result was still a major loss because restructuring and asset impairments were expensive.

But underneath those headline losses, the operating picture was already changing.

### PART 3 — THE NUMBER THAT MATTERED
Before special items, financial income, expenses and tax, LEGO improved from a DKK 1.061 billion loss in 2003 to a DKK 103 million profit in 2004.

That swing was not magic.

The company reported more than DKK 1 billion in cost savings from its action plan.

In other words, the turnaround was visible before the final profit number looked healthy.

That is a useful business lesson.

Restructuring can make a bad year look even uglier because the company finally recognizes the cost of old decisions.
### PART 4 — WHY FOCUS BEAT EXPANSION
The important strategic change was not simply "cut spending."

It was deciding what LEGO had to be good at.

A turnaround fails when management cuts everything evenly. That preserves the same confused strategy, only with fewer people and less money.

LEGO instead moved toward a simpler idea: protect the core play system, improve the economics around it, and remove activities that distracted capital and management attention.

That made the company smaller in complexity before it became larger in value.

### PART 5 — THE LONG PAYOFF
A decade later, the scale of the recovery was visible.

In 2014, LEGO reported revenue of DKK 28.6 billion and operating profit of DKK 9.7 billion.

That does not mean every later success came from one decision in 2004.

It means the company survived long enough for product innovation, global expansion and brand strength to matter again.

The first job of the turnaround was not growth.

It was creating a business capable of growing without collapsing under its own structure.

### ENDING — PAYOFF
The popular version of a turnaround story usually has one brilliant idea.

The real version is often less glamorous.

Stop doing things that do not earn their complexity.

Make the economics visible.

Protect the part of the business customers actually care about.

And accept that fixing old mistakes can make the short-term numbers look worse before they get better.

LEGO did not escape its crisis by building a bigger empire.

It escaped by deciding what deserved to remain at the center of one.
